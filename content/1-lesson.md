## What is digital storytelling?

Digital storytelling is the practice of using digital tools and media to tell a story. As a digital storyteller, you will combine text, images, video, audio, maps, and interactive features to communicate in ways that you simply can't with text alone. 


**[When Rains Fell in Winter](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/b63ced1af26a435bbfd4a910c8fac488){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}** is a StoryMap about a climate disaster in northwest Russia, where unseasonable winter rains killed 60,000 reindeer and devastated local herding communities. 

This event could be communicated many ways -- a written report of the basic facts, or a map showing amount of rainfall relative to previous years. But instead it's a story. It has characters rooted in a location, hard data mixed with first-hand testimonials. Along the way, a series of evocative images drive home the sense of loss this community experienced. Reading through this StoryMap, you don't just learn what happened, you begin to understand the human consequences as well. That's something that sticks with people. 

Throughout this session, return to this question as a guiding principle: what does the digital format empower me to do that I cannot with a research paper or presentation?

## What makes a good digital story?

A good digital story shares many of the same qualities as any other kind of story:
- **Characters and setting:** who is affected, and where?
- **Stakes or impact:** what is at risk, or what has been lost?
- **Narrative framing:** what throughline connects your evidence?
- **Resolution or call to action:** where does your audience end up after your story is through?

Digital stories differ from other kinds of stories primarily in their design. The choices you make about what media to include and where, how to pace your narrative, what interactive features to employ and why are what will take your story to another level.

With your neighbor, check out [Nine steps to great storytelling](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/429bc4eed5f145109e603c9711a33407){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}, a guide to digital storytelling principles put together by Esri. This guide is primarily intended for use with StoryMaps, and is actually a StoryMap itself, but most principles are applicable to any kind of digital story. While you browse, think about these questions: 
- How do these principles enhance a viewer's experience?
- Where have I seen these principles at work in the past?
- How can I apply these principles to my own research?

## Audience and purpose

Your absolute first step in creating a digital story **must** be to answer these two questions:

1. **Who is this for?**
Answers to this could include, but aren't limited to:
  - A general audience with no prior knowledge of your subject
  - A scholarly audience who may be familiar with your field but not your specific research and findings
  - A specific community whose story you're telling

2. **What outcome do you want for them when they're done?**
Answers to this could include: 
  - Understand an issue they hadn't encountered before
  - Take action or change their behavior
  - Experience something from another perspective

Your answers to these questions should drive every design decision you make, including what tool, platform or format you use, how much context to give, the media you choose, and more.

## The tool landscape

Today you'll work with two tools that represent different approaches to 
digital storytelling.

**ArcGIS StoryMaps**
- Part of the ArcGIS Online suite of tools
- Linear scrolling narrative combining text, media, and interactive maps
- Lives on Esri's servers — easy to share, requires an account to create
- Best for: research communication, public engagement, geographically-grounded narratives

  **Examples:**
  - [ISU Museums Art on Campus Tour](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/54d860a8a2284111a379f0a0c893fffc){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"} — Walking tour
  - [Walking China's Antique Roads](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/5f500ef24dc1457586e337b2edc53793){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"} — Travelogue
  - [Nature of the Book](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/ccbf360c22d74439a45bd1ef398ed170){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"} — Exhibit
  - [Finding Dubuque](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/d70a04b134e844569c84df77a6d20882){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"} — Historic preservation
  - [Our Food Story](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/collections/15788592cf844121a71a6a7dfc4cfc17){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"} — Memoir and cultural storytelling

**Twine**
- Browser-based, minimal coding required
- Nonlinear, branching narratives — the reader makes choices that shape the story
- Output is a standalone HTML file you can host anywhere
- Best for: interactive essays, simulations, perspective-taking, decision-making scenarios

  **Examples:**
  - [Cat Petting Simulator 2014](https://neongrey.itch.io/pet-that-cat) — Just for fun
  - [The Temple of No](https://crowscrowscrows.itch.io/the-temple-of-no) — Enhanced with audio
  - [Medusa's Laughter](https://mkchestnut.wixsite.com/medusas-laughter) — Interactive essay
  - [First Day in the Office](https://gkirilloff.itch.io/first-day-in-the-office) — Creating personal investment
  - [1984 Interrogation Scene](https://philome.la/wigardju/1984-sample-scene/play/index.html) — Adapting existing works
